The classic source

Carreño on the formal meal

Long before business lunches existed, a Venezuelan teacher wrote down how to behave at a formal table. Most of his rules still work. This page explains what he said about conduct at the meal, the objects on the table, and the way to treat servers and companions.

1. Who Carreño was

Manuel Antonio Carreño (Caracas, 1812 – Paris, 1874) was a Venezuelan musician, teacher and diplomat.[5] In 1853 he published, in installments, his Manual de urbanidad y buenas maneras (Manual of Civility and Good Manners). It spread quickly across Latin America, and people still say “the Carreño manual” when they mean good manners in general.[5]

About the edition we use

We read the Compendio, the shorter version that Carreño himself prepared for schools, in the 26th edition printed in Lima by Benito Gil in 1875 and digitized by the Library of Congress.[3] The rules below come from Chapter V, Article III, “De la mesa” (On the table), pages 100 to 114. Spanish quotations keep the spelling of that edition, such as “á” and “jamas”.

2. How to behave at a formal meal (lunch and dinner)

Carreño gives one set of rules for every formal meal. His chapter does not separate lunch from dinner, so everything here applies to both. He opens by saying that the table is one of the places where a person’s education shows most clearly and most quickly (p. 100).[3]

Posture

  • Sit at a comfortable distance from the table, neither too close nor too far, and lean forward only as much as you need (p. 101).[3]
  • Never put your elbows on the table or rest your whole forearm on it (pp. 101–102).[3]
  • Do not lean back in your chair or stretch out your legs (p. 102).[3]

Eating and drinking

  • Hold the spoon and the knife in your right hand. The knife is for cutting, never for carrying food to your mouth (pp. 102–103).[3]
  • Eat quietly: mouth closed, no slurping, no blowing on hot food, and no bites so large that you cannot speak (p. 105).[3]
  • Do not drink while you still have food in your mouth, and wipe your lips before and after drinking (p. 107).[3]
  • If you dislike something, say nothing. Leave bones and similar bits discreetly at the side of your plate (p. 106).[3]
  • If you must cough or sneeze, turn away from the food and draw as little attention as possible (p. 108).[3]

Pace and order

Keep the same rhythm as the rest of the table. Eating fast looks greedy, and eating slowly leaves you eating alone at the end (p. 104).[3] Soup comes first, main dishes and salads later, and dessert moves from fresh fruit to sweets (p. 110).[3] Stay seated until the person presiding over the table stands up (p. 109).[3]

Modern complement

A formal table today has more pieces than Carreño lists: a salad fork and a dinner fork on the left, a bread plate at the upper left, a dessert fork or spoon above the plate, and separate water and wine glasses at the upper right.[2][6] His two position rules still hold: bread on the left, drinks on the right. 4. How to treat servers

Carreño’s rule is simple: never speak harshly to the people who serve you, even when they make a serious mistake (p. 108).[3]

  • Do not scold them, however serious the error in the service (p. 108).[3]
  • Do not stand up or reach across another person to get something. Ask the servers, or the neighbor who has it close at hand (p. 102).[3]
  • Help them do their work: leave your fork and knife together on the plate so they know when to clear it (p. 107).[3]
  • When someone serves you, thank them in a few words with a slight nod (p. 114).[3]
Historical note

Carreño writes “sirvientes” (servants) because he describes formal meals in private homes with domestic staff. We translate the idea as “servers” or “waitstaff,” the people who do that work in a restaurant today.

Today: say “please” and “thank you”;[6] mention an allergy or a special request quietly instead of making it a scene;[4] and do not let the server take your plate while your companions are still eating.[4]

5. How to treat your companions

For Carreño, good manners at the table mean never making the people around you uncomfortable.

  • Let others go first. Do not sit down before your parents or anyone of higher standing (p. 101), and do not get up before the person presiding (p. 109).[3]
  • Respect their space. Do not lean on your neighbor’s chair, touch their arms with yours or reach in front of them (p. 102).[3]
  • Keep the talk pleasant. No arguments, and no news of illness, death or misfortune at the table (pp. 108–109).[3]
  • Do not pressure anyone. Offer a dish or a drink once. A second invitation is not a kindness (p. 113).[3]
  • Share fairly. When you serve from a common dish, count the guests so that it reaches everyone, and keep portions moderate (p. 113).[3]
  • Do not pass on what you have used. Never offer food from your plate, bread you have handled or a glass you have drunk from (p. 104).[3]

Today: the same care shows in newer habits. Keep your phone off the table, let your guest order first, and start with conversation rather than business.[1][4] See how to behave during the meal.

6. Carreño vs. today

Most of the manual has aged well. A few rules have relaxed, and one reflects the gender roles of its time rather than good manners.

Why we flag it

The rule about serving ladies belongs to the nineteenth century. We report it because it is in the text, not because we recommend it. The modern equivalent is to look after every guest equally.
