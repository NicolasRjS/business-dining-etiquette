During the meal

At the Table

Two things make a business meal feel easy: knowing which fork, plate and glass are yours, and keeping the attention on the conversation instead of on your food. This page covers both.

The place setting, piece by piece

One idea explains most of the table: start with the utensils farthest from your plate and work inward as each course arrives.[2][6] Select a numbered item to see what it is and how to use it.

Select a number on the drawing, or use the buttons below to step through all ten items.

  1. Napkin

    Once everyone is seated, unfold it and lay it on your lap.[2] If you step away, leave it on your chair. It returns to the table only when the meal is over.[4][6]

    Carreño, 1875 edition The napkin has a single purpose: wiping your lips.[3]

  2. Salad fork

    Forks sit to the left of the plate.[2] The outer fork is for the first course, which is often the salad, because you work from the outside in.[6]

  3. Dinner fork

    The fork closest to the plate is for the main course. Working inward means that, when the main dish arrives, this is the next fork in line.[2]

  4. Dinner plate

    The center of your place. When you pause, rest the fork and knife on the plate in a V shape. When you finish, lay them side by side on the plate. Used silverware never goes back on the tablecloth.[6]

    Carreño, 1875 edition Fork and knife together on the plate, handles toward you, tell the servers that you have finished.[3]

  5. Knife

    Knives go to the right of the plate.[2] Like every other utensil, once you have used it, it rests on the plate and not on the table.[6]

    Carreño, 1875 edition Never bring the knife to your mouth. He calls it a serious fault.[3]

  6. Spoon

    Spoons sit on the right, next to the knife.[2] If the soup is too hot, stir it to cool it. Do not blow on it.[6]

    Carreño, 1875 edition He lists blowing on hot food and slurping soup among the rude habits at the table.[3]

  7. Bread plate

    Yours is the one at the upper left.[6] A simple memory aid: solids on the left, liquids on the right.[2] Break off one piece of bread, butter it and eat it before you break off the next.[6]

    Carreño, 1875 edition Bread always goes on the left and is broken by hand, never cut with the knife.[3]

  8. Dessert fork and spoon

    The fork or spoon lying above your plate is for dessert.[6] Leave them where they are until dessert is served.

  9. Water glass

    Your drinks are at the upper right of your place.[6] If you are unsure which glass is yours, remember that liquids are on the right.[2]

    Carreño, 1875 edition Glasses and cups always stay on the right. Wipe your lips before and after you drink.[3]

  10. Wine glass

    It stands with the water glass at the upper right.[6] At a business meal, order alcohol only if your host does, and stop at one or two drinks.[6] Some career offices advise students to skip alcohol completely.[2]

The Carreño notes come from a manners manual first published in 1853. See what Carreño says about formal meals and how it compares with today.

How to behave during the meal

The goal of a business meal is a good conversation. Nina Zagat, co-founder of the Zagat restaurant guides, says guests should leave having enjoyed themselves and knowing you better as a person.[4] Every rule below serves that goal.

Order discreetly

Do not make your order the center of attention. If you have an allergy or a dietary restriction, mention it quietly to the server.[4]

  • Skip dishes that are hard to eat neatly, such as lobster or spaghetti.[4]
  • Avoid the most expensive item on the menu.[2] The price-matching rule tells you what to pick instead.
  • A half portion, or sharing a starter or a dessert, is perfectly acceptable.[4]

Napkin

The napkin goes on your lap when everyone is seated and stays there through dessert.[2][6] If you need to step away, say "excuse me" and leave the napkin on your chair, not on the table.[4][6] Use it only to wipe your lips.[3]

Pacing and plates

Wait for your host to signal that it is time to start eating.[2][6] Then match the speed of the table: eat neither quickly nor too slowly.[3]

  • Do not let the server take your plate until your companions have finished too.[4]
  • If the others skip the last course, skip it as well.[4]

Phone

Keep your phone off the table and make sure it does not ring.[4] Set every device to silent before you sit down.[6] A quick look at your notes or email is acceptable, as long as it stays quick.[4]

Wine

If you are choosing the wine, never pick the most expensive bottle. Ask the sommelier for a low to mid-priced option within your limit.[4]

  • Wine lists are usually organized by type or region and often suggest pairings. When in doubt, take the recommended wine.[1]
  • If the server hands you the cork, smell it gently. Say something only if it smells like vinegar.[1]
  • Red wine benefits from about 15 minutes of air before drinking.[1]
  • As a guest, order alcohol only if your host does, and limit yourself to one or two drinks.[6]

Conversation and when to talk business

Begin with small talk and learn what interests the other person. Do not push business in the first minutes, but do not wait until dessert to raise your serious points either. End the meal on a casual note.[4]

Most topics are fine as long as the discussion does not become heated.[4] Carreño was stricter: no arguments and no news of illness, death or misfortune at the table.[3]

Servers

Say "please" and "thank you" to the people serving you.[6] How you treat the staff is part of the impression you leave. Carreño made the same point in the nineteenth century: read how he says servers should be treated.
